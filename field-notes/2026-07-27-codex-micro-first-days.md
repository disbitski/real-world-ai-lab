## Summary

Codex Micro did not replace my keyboard, screen, or existing Codex habits.

It did something more specific and, for my nighttime workflow, more useful: it
gave parallel agents a physical presence in the room.

I often build on weekends and after work from a recliner, with my Mac Studio
connected to a 65-inch TV, a lapboard keyboard across my lap, a PS5 under the
screen, and cool blue lighting around me. I may be playing a game while Codex
runs several tasks. Before Codex Micro, I set timers or periodically switched
back to check which agents were finished, waiting, or asking for me.

Now I can glance at the light.

The six agent keys map to the projects I already keep pinned. Their state
colors tell me when work is moving or needs attention. The dedicated voice key
opens a live conversation inside Codex. Together, those two ideas made Codex
feel less like an application I had to monitor and more like an active part of
the room where I create.

That is the real value I found in this first-generation device. It turned
software state into ambient awareness, then made the path from thought to
conversation tactile.

## Observation

I discovered Codex Micro during OpenAI Build Week, while Codex and I were
building [Morrowward](2026-07-21-openai-build-week-four-days.md). I saw a post
on X, followed it to the OpenAI Supply page, and immediately preordered the
Clicky version for $230 before shipping.

I knew that price would not make sense for everyone. A builder could recreate
pieces of this workflow with a cheaper programmable keypad, lights, scripts, or
notifications.

But I have spent much of my career around consumer electronics. At Microsoft I
worked around Windows Phone, Kinect, and Xbox. At Amazon I helped launch Fire
tablets, Fire Phone, Fire TV, Echo, and Alexa developer experiences. I have
spent a lot of Januarys at CES. I still love first-generation devices because
they make a new interaction model concrete before the rest of the world has
decided whether it matters.

I also appreciate the craft of a finished object: packaging, weight, switches,
materials, setup, and the small decisions that make hardware feel intentional.
I keep first-generation phones and tablets, a Solana Seeker, and a SuiPlay
device for the same reason. Codex Micro was both useful equipment for the way I
already work and a small piece of AI-computing history I wanted to experience
firsthand.

## Why It Matters

Agentic work creates a new interface problem.

When one human collaborates with several concurrent agents, the hard part is
not always issuing another instruction. It is maintaining awareness:

- Which task is still running?
- Which task completed?
- Which thread has unread work?
- Which agent is blocked on my judgment?
- Which project should I return to next?

A screen can show all of that, but only when I am looking at the right screen.
A notification can interrupt me, but interruption is not the same as
awareness.

Codex Micro explores a third option: make agent state ambient, physical, and
selectable.

That matters beyond this one device. As agents keep working while I read,
think, build, or play, the best interfaces may not demand more attention. They
may help me stay loosely aware until my attention is actually needed.

## A Device For The Room Where I Actually Build

My daytime studio is the space people see in my Chainlink videos. It is where I
work from home, record, present, and spend most of my professional day.

My Codex time has another rhythm.

At night, I move into a darker gaming setup. The Mac Studio feeds a 65-inch TV.
I lean back in a recliner with a lapboard. The PS5 sits below the display. This
is where I explore game ideas, build Embermere, work on personal apps, write
field notes, and let GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra with Fast mode keep moving while I shift
between coding and gaming.

happens.

## Codex Intent, Work Louder DNA

The OpenAI branding is obvious, but so is the Work Louder foundation underneath
it.

OpenAI describes Codex Micro as a command center designed with Work Louder. The
official specifications list 13 mechanical switches, a touch sensor, rotary
encoder, planar joystick, RGB lighting, Bluetooth and USB-C connectivity, and
both ChatGPT Codex and Work Louder Input as software surfaces.

Once I opened Work Louder's Input app, the broader design became clearer.
Codex Micro is a tailored Codex controller, but it is not a sealed appliance.
Input exposes layers, keymaps, app-linked presets, backlight, and underglow
controls. The available presets I saw included Figma, Photoshop, Premiere,
DaVinci Resolve, and Blender.

## Power, Pair, Permit

Setup was simple.

The Clicky model has a battery, rear power button, USB-C port, and Bluetooth.
When I first powered it on, the blue light blinked and Codex Micro appeared
immediately in macOS Bluetooth settings.

![A cropped macOS Bluetooth panel showing Codex Micro connected with its battery level.](assets/2026-07-27-codex-micro-first-days/bluetooth-pairing.webp)

Codex then welcomed me with a dedicated two-step tour.

![The Codex Micro welcome screen explaining the OpenAI and Work Louder collaboration and requesting Input Monitoring permission.](assets/2026-07-27-codex-micro-first-days/codex-welcome.webp)

On macOS, ChatGPT needed Input Monitoring permission to respond to physical key
presses. That is a meaningful system permission, so I want to name it rather
than let easy setup make it invisible.

The Codex settings then exposed connection, battery, brightness, auto-dim,
agent-key behavior, dial behavior, and microphone-key behavior in one place.
My lights auto-dim after three minutes and wake when I interact with the
device or an agent state changes.

![Codex settings showing connection, battery, granted Input Monitoring, brightness, auto-dim, pinned agent keys, and composer navigation.](assets/2026-07-27-codex-micro-first-days/codex-micro-settings.webp)

In these first two days, I charged the device once. That is an early anecdote,
not a battery-life benchmark. The more useful point is that Bluetooth made the
controller practical in a recliner setup without adding another cable across
the room.

## Six Threads Became Muscle Memory

The six illuminated agent keys can follow pinned chats, recent chats, priority
chats, or custom assignments.

![Codex Micro settings showing choices for pinned, recent, priority, or custom agent-key assignments.](assets/2026-07-27-codex-micro-first-days/agent-key-modes.webp)

I chose the first six pinned chats.

That worked because I had already organized my projects in order of importance
and use: Fast Thirteen, Embermere, field notes and social work, investment
agents, and the rest of my active pinned work. I already knew what positions
one through six meant.

The hardware did not ask me to invent a new system. It inherited a system I had
already made useful. Within a short time, selecting the right project became
muscle memory, and the selected key remained visibly active.

This may be the most reusable lesson in the device:

> A physical control surface becomes intuitive when it maps to stable
> organization that already exists.

If my pinned order changed every hour, six unlabeled glowing keys would create
more cognitive load. Because the mapping is stable, the hardware makes the
organization faster to inhabit.

## The Room Can Tell Me What Codex Is Doing

Work Louder and OpenAI describe the agent states as idle, thinking, complete,
needs input, and error. In my own use, I experienced the colors this way:

| What I saw | What it meant in my workflow |
| --- | --- |
| White | The selected thread was idle. |
| Blue | Codex was processing work. |
| Green | Work or a result was ready for me to read. |
| Amber or yellow | The thread needed my attention or decision. |
| Red | The documented error state, which I thankfully have not seen yet. |

Each agent key can reflect its assigned task, while the diffused body lighting
makes the selected thread especially visible in my room.

The colors are not the work. They are a low-bandwidth signal about where my
attention belongs. That is enough.

## What I Actually Used

The default command keys put common Codex actions under my fingers: Auto,
Approve, Deny, Branch, and Voice.

Branch felt immediately natural because it mirrors the existing Codex control.
When an idea deserves a short detour without derailing the current thread, I can
branch physically and keep moving.

The dial can move through composer controls such as adding context, changing
approval mode, selecting the model, adjusting reasoning, and choosing speed. It
creates a visual highlight around those controls in Codex.

I have not needed it much.

Most of the time I remain in Approve for me with GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra and Fast
mode, so my stable defaults are already faster than turning a dial.

The joystick can surface recent skills. I have not used that much either. My
brain already reaches for the `$` command and the skill names I created.

That is not a criticism of the hardware. It is useful evidence about physical
interfaces:

- Ambient state solved a real problem for me.
- Stable thread keys quickly became muscle memory.
- Branching mapped to a behavior I already used.
- Controls that duplicate settled defaults or strong keyboard habits added
  less value.

The best shortcut is not the one with the most impressive control. It is the
one that removes friction from a repeated decision.

## The VoiceFirst Future Reached Coding

The dedicated voice key is where this story became personal.

I spent six years in the Alexa world talking about a **#VoiceFirst** future.
We imagined a time when English could become a programming language and the
distance from idea to implementation would collapse into conversation between
humans and machines.

For years, that idea was easier to demonstrate in smart homes than in software
development.

Then I pressed the voice key on Codex Micro.

The key can be configured for Push to talk or Voice Chat. I prefer Voice Chat:
I tap once, wait about a second for GPT's visual presence to appear inside
Codex, and start talking. I can have a natural back-and-forth without holding
the key. When I am done, I hold the voice key and the conversation closes.

| Voice-key behavior in Codex | GPT-Live present inside my Codex thread |
| --- | --- |
| ![Codex Micro voice settings showing Push to talk and Voice Chat behavior.](assets/2026-07-27-codex-micro-first-days/voice-mode-options.webp) | ![Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra selected and the blue GPT voice presence active in the thread.](assets/2026-07-27-codex-micro-first-days/gpt-live-codex.webp) |

I initially assumed the large microphone key meant the Micro had its own
microphone and speaker. It does not work that way in my setup. The device is the
controller; my Mac and connected AirPods provide the audio path.

In practice, that separation is better for me. AirPods make the conversation
private, close, and natural while the physical key gives me an obvious way to
start, mute, and end it.

OpenAI's current Voice experience is powered by GPT-Live. OpenAI describes
full-duplex interaction, natural pauses, interruptions, better listening, and
delegation to deeper models or agents while the conversation continues. Its
Help Center also says eligible desktop accounts can use Voice inside Codex with
the tools and permissions available there.

That matches what changed for me.

It does not feel like dictation.

Dictation makes me compose a command, speak it into a text box, check the
transcription, and submit it. Voice Chat gives me pacing, turn-taking, tone,
clarification, interruption, and response. I can think out loud, hear the idea
come back differently, and refine it in motion.

## A Conversation Can Change The Work

One of my first voice sessions was about a language-learning app I am
considering.

I did not arrive with a finished specification. GPT and I talked through the
idea. I explored what the experience could feel like, what might make it
different, and where the product should begin. The conversation itself became
part of the design process.

It felt more intimate than typing. It was such a different experience that I
almost felt like we needed to get to know each other a little first.

That feeling is real as an experience. It is not proof that a model is a person,
has feelings, or possesses a soul. Voice adds social texture: timing, tone,
acknowledgment, and the familiar rhythm of another voice responding. Those cues
can deepen presence and projection at the same time.

I have written before about that boundary in
[Claude's J-Space And The Wise Old Man](2026-07-06-claude-j-space-wise-old-man.md).
Resonance can be meaningful without becoming technical evidence for
consciousness.

The practical lesson is still hopeful. A more human communication channel can
help me express incomplete ideas that would be awkward to compress into a
perfect prompt. I just need to remain aware of why the channel feels different.

## Who This Is For

At $230 before shipping, Codex Micro is not a required part of using Codex.

I would consider it most compelling for someone who:

- regularly runs several Codex threads or agents
- benefits from seeing status without reopening the app
- has stable pinned projects or workflows to map to physical keys
- uses voice frequently
- values mechanical controls and industrial design
- enjoys customizing hardware beyond its default integration
- understands the appeal of a first-generation device exploring a new
  interaction model

I would be more cautious if someone expects it to make the model itself better,
replace a full keyboard, include a built-in mic and speaker, or automatically
transform habits that are not already organized.

The product's value depends on the surrounding workflow. Mine happened to fit
it unusually well.

## What I Should Watch

These are first impressions after only a few days, not a long-term review.

- I have not yet seen the red error state in normal work.
- My battery observation covers two days, not a controlled endurance test.
- I have not tested Blender or other Work Louder creative presets yet.
- The dial and recent-skills joystick currently duplicate habits that are
  already efficient for me.
- Input Monitoring is a real macOS permission that should be granted
  intentionally.
- The Micro does not replace a microphone, speaker, screen, keyboard, or the
  judgment required to supervise agents.
- Voice can make collaboration feel more personal. I should distinguish that
  felt intimacy from claims about the model's inner experience.
- A stable one-through-six mapping works for me because my pinned-project order
  was already disciplined.

The interesting question is not whether every control is useful on day one. It
is whether the device keeps reducing context switches after the novelty wears
off.

## Evaluation Ideas

- Over a week, how often does a color glance prevent me from manually checking
  Codex?
- How quickly can I identify the state of all six pinned threads without
  opening their windows?
- How often do I select the wrong thread, and does that rate change as muscle
  memory develops?
- Which physical controls become habitual after 30 days, and which remain
  decorative?
- Does Voice Chat help me express early, incomplete product ideas more clearly
  than typing?
- Do spoken sessions produce different decisions, more exploration, or simply
  more words?
- Does the device reduce disruptive notifications while still bringing blocked
  agents back to my attention?
- Does the value remain after the first-generation-hardware excitement fades?
- Can a Blender or Embermere layer add real cross-application value without
  making the Codex layer harder to remember?

## Working Principle

The best physical AI interface does not demand more attention. It turns the
right software state into ambient awareness, gives repeated decisions muscle
memory, and lets conversation begin at the speed of thought.
